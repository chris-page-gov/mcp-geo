# North Warwickshire — Priority 2 Assessment
## WHIMPLE Series / A444, A5, A47 Corridor

> [!warning] Priority 2 — High
> **North Warwickshire: Atherstone, Nuneaton, Coleshill corridor**
> Dominant series: WHIMPLE 3 (9.05%) + WHIMPLE 2 (7.42%) — combined ~21% of zone
> Primary hazard: **Shrink-swell moderate** (Triassic Mercia Mudstone); subgrade waterlogging
> Network at risk: A444, A5 (Watling Street), A47; rural C-class roads
> Recommended actions: Reactive maintenance pattern review | Drainage maintenance prioritisation

---

## 1. Zone Delineation

The North Warwickshire zone covers the upper county, broadly:

- **South**: Coventry northern fringe / M6 corridor (52.40°N)
- **North**: Tamworth / Staffordshire border (52.65°N)
- **West**: M42 / Coleshill corridor (~-1.75°W)
- **East**: Leicestershire border / Hinckley (~-1.30°W)

Key settlements: Nuneaton, Atherstone, Coleshill, Bedworth, Polesworth, Mancetter, Hartshill.

The zone is crossed by major A-road infrastructure on Triassic geology:
- **A444**: Coventry → Nuneaton → Burton upon Trent (N–S)
- **A5 (Watling Street)**: Roman road; Tamworth → Atherstone → Hinckley (E–W); runs on the geological boundary between Triassic Mercia Mudstone and Carboniferous Coal Measures
- **A47**: Rugby → Hinckley corridor (E–W)

**Assessment bbox**: `[-1.85, 52.40, -1.30, 52.65]`

The WHIMPLE series reflects in-situ Triassic Mercia Mudstone (Keuper Marl) with a drift veneer — reddish-brown, dense, moderately plastic clay subsoil.

## 4. NSI Field Evidence — WHIMPLE Profile

*Source: `landis_nsi_profile_summary(nsiId=8205)`, run 2026-04-07*
*Location: 52.516°N, -1.618°W (Mancetter / Hartshill area) | Altitude: 136m | Slope: 1° | Aspect: SE | Land use: permanent grassland*

| Horizon | Depth (cm) | Texture | Est. Clay % | Est. Silt % | Structure | Mottles | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ap | 0–10 | Humose clay loam | 28 | 31 | Mod. fine subangular | None | Very porous; many roots |
| Bw | 10–45 | Clay loam | 23 | 31 | Mod. medium subangular | Few (75YR4/2) | Non-calcareous; some gley |
| Bg | 45–70 | Clay | 43 | 40 | Mod. medium **angular** blocky | Few (5YR6/4) | Slightly porous; Fe-Mn nodules |
| Cg | 70–110 | Silty clay | 38 | 45 | **Massive** | Common (5YR5/3) | Very slightly porous; non-calcareous |

**Topsoil chemistry**: pH **4.3** (very acidic), CARBON 4.5%, clay 24.7%, silt 30.2%, sand 45.1%

**Key diagnostic features — engineering interpretation:**

The WHIMPLE profile differs critically from the Feldon EVESHAM/DRAYTON profiles in three ways:

1. **Lower peak clay content** — 43% vs 54% for EVESHAM. Above the 35% vertic threshold but less plastic; shrink-swell amplitude is moderate not active.

2. **No strongly developed vertic Bw horizon** — the 45–70cm horizon shows only *moderately* angular blocky structure, not the *strongly* developed structure of EVESHAM. This means less crack development and lower volumetric shrinkage on drying.

3. **Reddish matrix (5YR Munsell = reddish brown)** throughout vs the grey/olive tones of Jurassic Lias. This reflects Triassic Mercia Mudstone origin — different mineral composition (less swelling clay minerals), different mechanical behaviour under load.

4. **Massive structure at 70–110cm** — parent Triassic mudstone; prone to weakening under cyclic loading rather than the angular blocky clay fabric of Lias.

5. **pH 4.3 topsoil** — strongly acidic on permanent grassland; indicates long-term leaching and slow turnover. This is characteristic of poorly drained WHIMPLE sites on gentler terrain.

6. **Common gley mottles from 70cm** (5YR5/3 = pinkish grey) — seasonally wet but not the severe gleying of BROCKHURST.

**NSI texture bulk data**: CLAY=24.7%, SILT=30.2%, M-fine sand=17.2% — the WHIMPLE topsoil is substantially sandier than the Feldon clays. This reduces plastic shrinkage but also means the soil is more susceptible to surface capping (dense surface crust on rainfall impact), which affects drainage.

## 7. Risk Characterisation

### 7.1 Primary mechanism: subgrade waterlogging and moderate shrink-swell

The WHIMPLE series produces a distinctly different failure mode from the Feldon EVESHAM/DENCHWORTH:

- **Waterlogging at shallow depth** (gley from 10cm) saturates road subgrade during winter months — this is the dominant driver of pavement deterioration on WHIMPLE roads
- **Moderate shrink-swell** (43% clay peak, moderately angular blocky) produces less-severe but still real seasonal movement — more gradual pavement degradation than Feldon
- **Surface capping** on WHIMPLE clay loam (sandy fraction) creates impermeable crusts that concentrate runoff at road edges — lateral infiltration into subgrade
- **No landslip risk** — the Triassic parent material does not develop the slickensided failure planes of Jurassic Lias clay

### 7.2 Road network risk by type

| Road | Crossing WHIMPLE? | Risk level | Expected failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| A444 (Coventry–Nuneaton) | Partly — transitions across geology | 🟡 Moderate | Subgrade waterlogging in low-lying sections; drainage-sensitive |
| A5 (Watling Street) | Runs on Triassic/Carboniferous boundary | 🟠 High — geological boundary | Clay-to-sandstone transitions cause differential subgrade behaviour; cut-and-fill on WHIMPLE |
| A47 (Rugby–Hinckley) | Partially | 🟡 Moderate | Mixed subgrade; monitoring appropriate |
| Rural C-class across WHIMPLE plateau | Full WHIMPLE exposure | 🟠 High — worst maintenance:risk ratio | Thin pavement + seasonally waterlogged clay subgrade = progressive surface failure |

The A5 is the highest-priority individual route within this zone. As a Roman road following the geological boundary, its carriageway profile transitions between sandstone/gravel (stable) and Triassic clay (unstable) repeatedly over short distances. These transitions generate differential subgrade movement that is difficult to detect without systematic logging of maintenance interventions by USRN segment.

## 8. Recommended Actions

### 8.1 Reactive Maintenance Pattern Review ✅ METHOD DEFINED

**Status: Methodology defined. Execution requires PMS access.**

The reactive maintenance pattern review is the most efficient diagnostic for a zone where the risk is moderate and widespread. The recommended workflow:

**Step 1 — NATMAP overlay**
- Clip WHIMPLE 2 + WHIMPLE 3 polygons (from NATMAP Vector open data) to county road network by USRN
- Also include SALOP, RAGDALE, BECCLES 3, CLIFTON polygons — all impeded clay, similar risk profile
- Exclude BROMSGROVE, WICK, RIVINGTON, ARROW (lower risk)

**Step 2 — Extract reactive maintenance records by USRN**
- Pull pothole and patching records from PMS for past 5–10 years
- Aggregate cost/intervention by USRN segment
- Calculate reactive spend per lane-km per year as the base metric

**Step 3 — Correlate with soil risk**
- Compare USRN segments on WHIMPLE/impeded clay polygons vs BROMSGROVE/WICK (control)
- Expected outcome: WHIMPLE roads will show ~1.5–2.5× higher reactive spend per km than sandy/loamy roads of equivalent traffic count and age
- If correlation confirmed: validates soil-risk based prioritisation; if not confirmed: examine whether roads already have adequate drainage (e.g. legacy works)

**Step 4 — Flag outliers**
- Identify WHIMPLE roads with particularly high reactive spend (top quartile within the WHIMPLE stratum)
- These are candidates for drainage maintenance intervention rather than continued reactive repair
- Also identify any roads with unexpectedly low spend on WHIMPLE — these may have already-adequate drainage or may be under-reported

**Expected finding based on analogy with Lincolnshire**: Harrison et al. 2023 demonstrated that roads on poorly draining soils required up to 3× the maintenance spend of roads on well-drained soils of equivalent classification and age. North Warwickshire WHIMPLE roads on impeded clay are expected to show a similar (if less extreme) pattern.

### 8.2 Drainage Maintenance Prioritisation ✅ METHOD DEFINED

**Status: Methodology defined. Field work required.**

The drainage mechanism on WHIMPLE clay is different from Feldon — it is primarily about **surface water infiltration through cracked road surfaces** and **blocked roadside ditches preventing drawdown** of the seasonal water table. The key interventions:

**Immediate** (WHIMPLE roads with above-average reactive spend):
- Inspect roadside ditches and gully connections — clear blockages
- Assess culvert condition at all watercourse crossings on WHIMPLE polygons
- Check for cross-fall degradation — WHIMPLE clay roads often lose cross-fall through repeated patching, pooling water at edges

**12-month programme** (all WHIMPLE C-class roads):
- Systematic ditch-clearing across WHIMPLE polygon extent
- Identify locations where road acts as a dam across natural drainage — particularly on A5 (Watling Street) where the road embankment can block natural drainage across the Triassic clay

**5-year horizon**:
- Where reactive spend consistently exceeds lifecycle threshold on WHIMPLE C-class roads: consider surface-water drainage improvement (edge-of-road perforated drains; improved gully spacing) before next scheduled resurfacing
- Road design standard for next resurfacing on WHIMPLE: minimum 100mm granular sub-base (if not already present); ensure adequate cross-fall (2.5% minimum)
