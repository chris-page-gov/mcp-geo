## Slide 3 — The Stress Test: Lincolnshire as a National Case

### Why Lincolnshire is the Critical Test Case

**70%** of the UK's food produce originates in this region ('The Breadbasket'), relying entirely on a **single-lane network**. There is no redundancy — if the rural road network fails, the food supply chain fails with it.

**+1,134%** increase in national motor vehicle traffic between 1949 and 2019, heavily skewed toward heavy agricultural transport. The roads were never designed for this load.

**14th → 37th** — a precipitous drop for the UK in global road quality rankings between 2006 and 2019. The decline is measurable and accelerating.

> [!quote] THE STRESS TEST
> A 21st-century agricultural supply chain is being forced over an unreinforced, Roman-era drainage landscape.

The drainage ditches, Drainage Board infrastructure, and field drainage patterns in the Lincolnshire Fens are built on Roman land reclamation works. The load being placed on them in 2026 could not have been imagined when they were constructed.

|---|---|---|

| Below | Capping & Structural Layers | — |
| Base | Stable Foundational Soil & Bedrock | **Surface sits entirely at the mercy of the soil** |

> [!danger] THE STRUCTURAL REALITY
> 87% of Lincolnshire's network consists of minor roads (A, B, C, and unclassified) that evolved directly from historical mud tracks. Without modern foundations, they possess **no structural buffer** against shallow geological processes.

**What "zero foundation" means in practice:**
- Every load pulse from a passing HGV is transmitted directly and in full to the compressible subsoil
- There is no capping layer to distribute stress laterally
- The 25–200mm of fragmented surface asphalt is the entire structural package
- Settlement, compression, and shrinkage of the subsoil map directly onto the road surface with no attenuation

## Slide 5 — The Three Horizons: The Saturated Sponge

The three soil types that underlie most of the vulnerable Lincolnshire road network — all Holocene deposits, none naturally consolidated by overlying rock:

### Horizon 1: Peat (Organic Soils)
**Highly compressible.** Formed in low-energy fluvial environments. Experiences **massive volume reduction** when subjected to static load or rapid dewatering.

The key mechanical property: peat has very low shear strength and extremely high compressibility. It behaves like a sponge — it absorbs load by collapsing, and desiccation causes irreversible volume loss.

### Horizon 2: Tidal Flat Deposits
Silty clay with layers of sand and peat. Inherently soft with **high natural water content**. Highly susceptible to shrink-swell cycles as groundwater fluctuates.

The layered structure (silty clay + sand + peat interbeds) creates internal heterogeneity — different layers respond differently to loading and moisture change, producing differential settlement within a single deposit.

### Horizon 3: Alluvium
Silt and clay deposited in floodplains. Exhibits **localised heterogeneity**, creating dangerous zones of differential movement.

Alluvial deposits are laterally variable — point bars, channel fills, overbank deposits, and cut-offs all have different properties at sub-metre scale.

> [!note] THE SATURATED SPONGE
> Because these are relatively recent Holocene deposits, they have never been naturally consolidated by overlying rock. They retain high water content, waiting to be crushed or dried out.
>
> **Contrast with engineered ground:** A Victorian railway embankment resting on London Clay has been consolidating under load for 150 years. These fenland deposits have been under load only since the road was placed on them — and consolidation is ongoing.

## Slide 6 — The Climate & Anthropogenic Multiplier

### The Destructive Feedback Loop (5-Step Cycle)

```
① HOTTER/DRIER SUMMERS
   Climate baseline shift
         ↓
② SURGING AGRICULTURAL IRRIGATION
   Farmers extract water to save crops
         ↓
③ LOWERED DITCH WATER LEVELS
   Drainage Boards drop levels to meet demand
         ↓
④ SOIL MOISTURE DEFICIT SPIKES
   Sub-base dries out aggressively
         ↓
⑤ VOLUMETRIC SHRINKAGE
   Ground physically shrinks and pulls away
         ↓
   back to ① (next summer is drier still)
```

> [!danger] SYSTEMIC INSIGHT
> The impermeable road surface seals moisture in the centre, but the road edges are exposed to aggressive drying from parallel drainage ditches. This artificial dehydration creates a **lethal moisture gradient** that literally pulls the asphalt apart from the outside in.

**Why this is a multiplier, not just an additive:**
- Climate change (step 1) increases the amplitude of summer moisture deficits
- Agricultural irrigation demand (step 2) rises in response, extracting more groundwater
- Drainage Boards respond to irrigation demand (step 3) by lowering ditch levels further
- Each step compounds the previous — it is not a linear relationship but an accelerating one
- The road edges and road centre experience fundamentally different moisture regimes because of the impermeable cap effect

> [!danger] THE REVELATION
> When deep-foundation 'A-Roads' are excluded from the dataset, the deterioration rate on GeoSure 'E' soil spikes catastrophically. These shallow evolved roads lose between **3 to 17 years of structural life every single year.**

**Reading the data:**
- The gap between class C (0 years/year) and class D (3.1 years/year) is non-linear — a threshold effect when peat and tidal flat deposits are encountered
- The range at class E (3–17 years/year) reflects the variety of evolved road structures — some have slightly more asphalt than others, but none have a real foundation
- A road losing 17 years of residual life per year has a theoretical structural life of less than 5 years from new

> [!quote] Without deep foundations, the lifespan of a surface layer is entirely dictated by the compressibility of the dirt beneath it.

**Implication for the ground resilience skill:** BGS `GeoSure_Compressible_Ground` class D or E on an unclassified or C-class road is the single highest-risk combination in the dataset. It must be flagged with the highest priority in any screening output.

## Slide 9 — Explanatory Visual: The Roddon Effect (Differential Settlement)

### The Three-Step Mechanism

**Step 1 — The Landscape:**
Road overlays flat peat (compressible, low bearing capacity) and a Roddon — a buried sandy riverbed deposit, stiffer and denser than the surrounding peat. From the road surface above, nothing is visible.

**Step 2 — The Compression:**
Heavy HGV loading combined with drought conditions creates compressive forces. The surrounding peat compresses under load. The Roddon remains rigid and unyielding — it is effectively incompressible relative to the peat.

**Step 3 — The Rupture:**
Differential settlement: peat compresses, Roddon does not. The road surface over the peat sinks, but the road surface over the Roddon stays put — creating a **subterranean speed bump**. Asphalt buckles and shatters at the interface. System failure.

> [!danger] THE RODDON EFFECT
> A rigid subsurface element causes catastrophic surface failure. The mechanism is entirely subsurface — no surface inspection can predict or detect it before failure. Only LiDAR (to identify the roddon ridge) or ground investigation can reveal the risk.

**Why LiDAR is essential here:** Roddons are visible on EA 1m LiDAR as subtle linear ridges 0.5–2m above the surrounding peat surface. Without LiDAR, the heterogeneity is invisible at any mapping scale. The LiDAR reference in the slide confirms this is the recommended detection method.

## Slide 10 — Explanatory Visual: The Edge Effect & Vegetation

### The Moisture Gradient Mechanism

The cross-section shows three competing moisture processes acting on the same road simultaneously:

**Left edge — The ditch effect:**
- Parallel drainage ditch → lowered water table
- Extreme dry zone in the subsoil beneath the verge
- Evaporation from exposed ditch banks
- Lateral tension on the road edge as soil contracts

**Centre — The impermeable cap:**
- Road surface is impermeable → moisture trapped underneath
- High moisture content maintained in sub-base centre
- Trapped water creates a hydrostatic pressure zone

**Right edge — The vegetation effect:**
- Tree root system with deep root suction
- Evapotranspiration draws moisture from the soil laterally and vertically
- Shrinkage force on the road edge as soil desiccates

**The failure:**
Both edges drying independently while the centre stays wet → lateral tension from both sides → **centre-line cracking**. The asphalt is literally being pulled in two simultaneously from opposite edges.

> [!danger] THE MECHANISM
> The road acts as an impermeable cap, preserving moisture in the centre. However, aggressive evapotranspiration from tree roots and lowered water tables in the deep ditch suck water from the edges. This severe lateral moisture gradient causes the edges to shrink and pull away, literally tearing the asphalt in half down the centre line.

**Field diagnosis:** A road showing centre-line cracking (not edge cracking) in a fen or floodplain setting with trees on one or both sides and parallel drainage ditches is displaying the edge effect. It is not a pavement failure — it is a soil moisture gradient failure.
